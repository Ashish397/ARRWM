\begin{abstract}
We adapt a pretrained Wan2.1 1.3B diffusion transformer (DiT) into an action conditioned world model using effectively unsupervised pedestrian real world data. World model literature largely relies on precise and accurate action labels obtained from video game controllers, driving logs, or camera rigs. An alternative family of methods choose to derive latent actions that explain changes from one latent frame to the next using complex models that lack cross context grounding. In this work we present a simple alternative - we derive our abstracted egomotion vector as principal components of simple, chunked, noisy pixel tracking outputs. PCA is linear, additive, and interpretable, offering us a myriad of benefits over more complex transformation spaces. We use teacher forcing to train multiple chunks (~0.5 second videos) simultaneously in the interest of efficiency. Decoding multiple videos and passing gradient through them is prohibitive in memory and invites the model to game the pixel tracking signal. Instead we introduce a 3D CNN based critic that learns to supervise egomotion entirely in the latent space. We train this online critic using localised samples aided by the frozen and PCA-abstracted pixel tracking pipeline.
After training for 5000 steps, we report a resultant $r>0.98$ action following score while largely preserving underlying scene content for videos of up to 5 seconds.
\end{abstract}

Video DiTs have recently found great success in generating high resolution video given some input conditions after having been trained on internet scale video for thousands of hours. While impressive, we naturally want to upgrade these models from video makers into world makers that adapt their generation to abstract egomotion inputs. World models have widely been hailed as the next level of intelligence leading to applications such as training agents / humans in silico, intelligent planning, and expansive digital twins. However, the scale of training that unsupervised training bought LLM models are hard to achieve with world models as they currently need meticulously labelled data to function, data that simply does not exist naturally - new data either needs to be painstakingly collected \cite{navwormod, cameracontrol2, cosmos2, uniwm, xworld, gaia2}, or training is relegated to the domain of videogames: \cite{gamengen2024, diamond, olafworld, mineworld, gamefactory, oasis2} synthesised worlds that can never cover the complexity and stochasticity of the real world. While some use latent action models to bridge the gap, these often prove to be complex models whose action spaces lack any meaningful grounding (cross context identifiability) \cite{genie, gamengen2024, olafworld, adaworld}. The open question thus is not whether this conversion of pretrained video models to world models is possible --- several recent systems demonstrate that it is --- but under what initial supervision this transition can be realised.

When it comes to egomotion world models, the nature of the data itself is a point of differentiation: videogame data offers handmade worlds with simple rules of interaction, cloned features, and an ever consistent rigid motion + camera representation. These data lead to the models struggling to produce the depth of texture present in the real world and are thus consigned to generating uncanny looking videos. Real world driving datasets are another popular approach that trade handmade environments for real roads but often involve backgrounds that evolve slowly with actors that behave predictably. Sometimes real world low speed data is also used \cite{navwormod} but it is often manually collected, limiting scale and inviting overfitting. They are also sometimes augmented with videogame data to provide grounded action supervision \cite{diffusionworldmodels}. Tellingly, even high performing systems built to handle navigation retreat from messy data when they can: a recent Wan based world model abandons real world walking and drone data for unreal engine generated data, because real egocentric motion is dominated by forward travel and when it is not, entangled across different dimensions ~\cite{relic}.

We use one of these messy real world datasets that prove to be insightful, brilliant, and interesting. Frodobots \cite{frodobots} consists of low speed pedestrian rover footage that operate in real cities across the globe. These rovers are teleoperated across vast distances, leading to the dataset coupled commands proving unreliable and significantly laggy. Realized egomotion also diverges from commanded egomotion when collisions, bad internet, and uneven ground are met. Scenes are often full of pedestrians and actors that behave in inexplicable ways that only the real world can produce; street furniture is complex, unique, and varied. In this work we train on Frodobots but we discard the noisy action labels provided as we find that it hurts WM training more than it helps. We instead assume the videos are unsupervised and recover action signals directly from pixels instead.

Doing so well rests on three simple ideas, that when adopted together we find materially improves WM training. First, we derive an interpretable action space directly from video motion: a frozen point tracker \cite{cotracker} over each chunk (lasting \~0.5 seconds) yields displacement fields whose principal components (PCA) form a linear, additive, and interpretable egomotion basis. We find that the two principal components that represent the highest variance recover the two control dimensions afforded by the ground robot. Prior flow- and track-conditioned generators recover motion from video too, but then go on to condition on raw, dense optical flow or pixel tracks~\cite{flovd, motionprompting, onlyflow}, an un-abstracted control surface that would require a translator to turn an abstract command such as go straight into a full motion field. Using PCA we sidestep the need for this complex conditioning and subsequent translation at the inference stage. 

At training we use the principal components of the pixel tracks to determine the abstracted egomotion directions and we use this to train the model, obtaining the best of both worlds. We perform this supervision without ever decoding to pixels: an action critic learns how to map the generated latents' motion to the two abstracted PCA dimensions and subsequently uses the same action critic to supervise the DiT such that it learns to produce the correct egomotion. This avoids the memory cost of passing decoder gradients for tens of pixel frames simultaenously while also avoiding the model cheating by learning to control just the pixels we track. 

We inject these egomotion commands simultaenously through two complementary conditioning pathways: AdaLN-zero and sideband action tokens. We show in our ablations that removing either degrades the model. AdaLN proves crucial in that ablating it removes the ability to influence the model, while action tokens are shown to make the whole model more robust and less prone to collapsing while actions become more expressive and powerful. To our knowledge, we are the first work that mixes AdaLN and action tokens as a form of dual conditioning, although dual conditioning at the time of writing has been used in two other works \cite{wovr, dexac}, albeit combining AdaLN with cross attention.

We note one simplification in our setting: our rover has no neck nor a free moving camera decoupled from its body, so camera motion and agent egomotion coincide, making our control space smaller than an embodiment with an independent viewpoint. The additive structure of our basis, however, does not exclude adding further degrees of freedom to it, which we leave to future work.

We therefore present a framework through which pretrained video DiTs can be transitioned into action following world models through the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}

\item An unsupervised, interpretable egomotion action space recovered from real video via point tracking and PCA, requiring no action labels and no learned latent action model, whose additive basis cleanly separates and recombines the often entangled real world motion.

\item An online latent egomotion critic that supervises action-following entirely in latent space, distilling PCA abstracted pixel tracks without backpropagating through the decoder or the tracker itself.

\item A dual conditioning scheme that injects egomotion through both AdaLN-Zero and sideband action tokens, where AdaLN carries crucial action information while action tokens provide valuable support by stabilising video generation and strengthening action response.

\end{enumerate}

We instantiate these ideas on 80 hours of Frodobots and adapt the Wan base model over five passes of this dataset. We evaluate by giving the model ground truth actions as well as flipped ground truth actions and assess both the quality and the action following. We also provide the outputs from a 360 degree rotation to show consistency.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{World models from pretrained video generators.}
While earlier literature focussed on training up world models from scratch \cite{mineworld, navwormod}, contemporary literature tends to focus on adapting pretrained video DiTs \cite{wan2025, cogvid, skyreels} into action-conditioned world models. Closest to our setting are recent autoregressive egomotion or camera-action systems such as minWM, WorldCam, Astra, Matrix-Game 2.0, HY-WorldPlay, and Yume, which demonstrate that pretrained video models can be converted into controllable rollout models when supplied with camera poses, keyboard/mouse controls, game actions, or curated action annotations~\cite{minwm, worldcam, astra, matrixgame2, hyworldplay, yume}. The work closest to the foundation-model style is Cosmos-Predict2.5, which augments large-scale video pretraining with action post-training on smaller robot and driving datasets~\cite{cosmos}. Other works \cite{dws, vid2world} train action conditioning modules against well supervised action labels. To ground their actions better, some turn to video games and synthesised worlds \cite{gamefactory, matrixgame2}, trading the complexity of the real world for the well behaved action space. Others turn to instrumented camera movement trajectories \cite{cameracontrol2} and instrumented physics quantities \cite{aeroworld} derived through an onboard IMU, resulting in much better behaved action spaces but relying heavily on instrumented data.

\paragraph{Latent action models}
An alternative family of methods remove labels by learning a latent action space from video via inverse dynamics~\cite{genie, lapo, lapa, adaworld}. These are powerful without supervision, but the learned codes tend to be opaque and poorly aligned across scenes: recent analysis notes that independently learned latent actions entangle scene-specific cues and lack a shared coordinate system across contexts~\cite{olafworld}.

\paragraph{Motion-conditioned generation from recovered motion.}
There also exist other work that attempt to use unsupervised video albeit with caveats. Most works in this space largely use overactuated pixel tracks \cite{motionprompt} or high detail optical flow \cite{flovd, onlyflow} that lead to difficulties when only abstracted motion is input - whole videos or track generators then need to be used to provide an input to the model in order for it to generate videos. Videos generated with these when underactuated inputs are provided often end up looking rigid where motion tracks are not given, and overall generalisation to extended egomotion (such as constant turning for 5 seconds) are not provided. These methods also largely target interpolation via in frame object motion rather than shifting entire poses that egomotion based conditioning affords, which forces extrapolation in both time and space as new plausible content is actively generated.

\paragraph{Conditioning and latent supervision.}
Works in this space largely use one of three different conditioning methods: cross attention, ControlNet style adapters, and AdaLN or RoPE style modulation. GameFactory and Matrix-Game 2.0 add explicit action modules inside the DiT blocks: continuous mouse or viewpoint actions are concatenated to latent features and processed with an MLP followed by temporal self-attention, while discrete keyboard actions are injected through cross-attention~\cite{gamefactory, matrixgame2}. Aero-World embeds its inertial controls with an MLP and injects them as a separate action-token stream through cross-attention~\cite{aeroworld}. Camera-control methods instead often use geometry-specific adapters or positional encodings, such as PRoPE/CPA-style camera-aware attention~\cite{prope, cpa} or Pl"ucker/feature branches~\cite{cameractrl, motionctrl}. We instead route our low-dimensional egomotion through the DiT's native AdaLN pathway together with sideband action tokens; the two prior world models we are aware of that use more than one action pathway pair AdaLN with cross-attention rather than tokens~\cite{wovr, dexac}. Our latent critic, finally, relates to guidance from auxiliary readouts: Aero-World is the closest, using a frozen latent probe for differentiable supervision without decoding~\cite{aeroworld}, however its probe is trained (on ground-truth IMU) in it's entirety \emph{before} it is used for supervision, whereas our action critic is trained online alongside the generator. Our action critic is trained against the frozen PCA abstracted pixel tracks. 
\section{Preliminaries}

\subsection{Flow Matching}

Let $p_0$ denote a simple base distribution, typically a standard Gaussian, and let $p_1$ denote the data distribution. Flow matching learns a time-dependent vector field that transports samples from $p_0$ to $p_1$ through an ordinary differential equation (ODE)
\begin{equation} 
    d x_t = v_\theta(x_t,t)\,dt, 
    \qquad t\in[0,1]. 
    \label{eq:flow_ode_differential} 
\end{equation}
where $x_0 \sim p_0$ and the terminal sample $x_1$ is distributed according to $p_1$ after integration.

The central object in flow matching is a probability path $\{p_t\}_{t\in[0,1]}$ connecting the base and data distributions. If $x_t \sim p_t$ evolves under a velocity field $u_t(x)$, then the density satisfies the continuity equation
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial p_t(x)}{\partial t}
    =
    - \nabla \cdot \left(p_t(x) u_t(x)\right).
    \label{eq:continuity}
\end{equation}
Flow matching trains a neural vector field $v_\theta$ to approximate $u_t$ by minimizing
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{t,x_t}
    \left[
        \left\|
            v_\theta(x_t,t) - u_t(x_t)
        \right\|_2^2
    \right].
    \label{eq:fm_loss}
\end{equation}
In practice, the marginal velocity $u_t(x)$ is usually not available in closed form. Conditional flow matching therefore constructs a tractable conditional path from paired variables and regresses the corresponding conditional velocity. Given samples $x_0 \sim p_0$ and $x_1 \sim p_1$, one defines an interpolation
\begin{equation}
    x_t = \psi_t(x_0,x_1),
    \label{eq:general_path}
\end{equation}
with target velocity
\begin{equation}
    u_t = \frac{d}{dt}\psi_t(x_0,x_1).
    \label{eq:general_velocity}
\end{equation}
The model is then trained using
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CFM}}
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{t,x_0,x_1}
    \left[
        \left\|
            v_\theta(x_t,t) - u_t
        \right\|_2^2
    \right].
    \label{eq:cfm_loss}
\end{equation}
Although the loss is written using conditional velocities, the optimal predictor recovers the corresponding marginal velocity through conditional expectation:
\begin{equation}
    v^\star(x,t)
    =
    \mathbb{E}
    \left[
        u_t
        \mid
        x_t=x
    \right].
    \label{eq:optimal_fm_velocity}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Rectified Flow}

Rectified flow is a particular instance of flow matching that uses straight-line interpolants between base samples and data samples. Given $x_0 \sim p_0$ and $x_1 \sim p_1$, the path is
\begin{equation}
    x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1.
    \label{eq:rf_path}
\end{equation}
The corresponding velocity is constant along each pairwise path:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d x_t}{dt} = x_1 - x_0.
    \label{eq:rf_velocity}
\end{equation}
The rectified-flow objective is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RF}}
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{t,x_0,x_1}
    \left[
        \left\|
            v_\theta((1-t)x_0+t x_1,t)
            -
            (x_1-x_0)
        \right\|_2^2
    \right].
    \label{eq:rf_loss}
\end{equation}
At sampling time, one draws $x_0 \sim p_0$ and integrates
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d x_t}{dt}=v_\theta(x_t,t)
\end{equation}
from $t=0$ to $t=1$. The resulting sample is
\begin{equation}
    x_1
    =
    x_0
    +
    \int_0^1 v_\theta(x_t,t)\,dt.
    \label{eq:rf_sample}
\end{equation}

The rectified-flow formulation is attractive because the training paths are linear, and the learned dynamics are encouraged to be as straight as possible. In contrast to diffusion models, which are often motivated by reversing a stochastic noising process, rectified flow directly learns a deterministic transport field. This does not require changing the output space of the model: the network always predicts a velocity in the same space as $x_t$.

\section{Method}

We adapt a pretrained Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B DiT into an egomotion conditioned world model using the FrodoBots dataset, which consists of real world data of a rover moving about in urban cityscapes. Our method has two parts: an unsupervised egomotion action space recovered from video (\S\ref{sec:pca}) and a latent critic that supervises egomotion without decoding (\S\ref{sec:critic}). The recovered action is injected into the generator through a combination of zero-initialised adaptive layer normalisation (AdaLN-Zero) and per-chunk action tokens (\S\ref{sec:planar}). Throughout, the model generates video autoregressively in short chunks, each chunk conditioned on the preceding context and a per-chunk action.

\subsection{Planar Action-Conditioned Rectified Flow}
\label{sec:planar}

While action conditioning has been engineered into video DiTs many times, it is rarely stated \emph{what} the action is expected to do to the learned flow. We give a minimal formalisation of action conditioning as a residual redirection of a context-conditioned flow. The formalisation is an analysis model, not an architectural constraint: it yields testable predictions---scene-independence, approximate linearity, and composability of the action's effect on the generative trajectory---which we test on the trained model rather than impose on it.

We consider a rover moving on a locally planar surface, so its controllable egomotion has two degrees of freedom: forward displacement and yaw. We condition generation on the data-derived action coordinates of \S\ref{sec:pca},
\begin{equation}
    a =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        a_{\mathrm{fwd}} \\
        a_{\mathrm{yaw}}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \in \mathbb{R}^2,
    \label{eq:planar_action}
\end{equation}
the two leading principal components of tracked image motion, which emerge aligned with forward displacement and yaw respectively. This action space is scene-independent by construction: the same $a$ denotes the same rover egomotion regardless of the visual scene, while scene-dependent image effects such as parallax, occlusion, and object-scale changes must be inferred from the visual context. Note that $a=0$ is not the \emph{absence} of control but a genuine command---remain stationary---and stationary segments are deliberately retained in the training distribution so that a zero command brakes the rover rather than letting the generator coast on its motion prior.

Let $c$ denote the conditioning context, a short history of frames, and $a$ the commanded action of Eq.~\eqref{eq:planar_action}. Let $t\in[0,1]$ be diffusion time, $x_0\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ the initial sample, and $x_t$ the state at time $t$. We write generation as a context- and action-conditioned flow
\begin{equation}
    d x_t = v(x_t,t,c,a)\,dt,
    \label{eq:conditioned_flow}
\end{equation}
which models plausible future frames under action control. The generated latent is recovered by integrating this flow from the initial sample,
\begin{equation}
    x_1
    =
    x_0
    +
    \int_0^1 v(x_t,t,c,a)\,dt,
    \label{eq:flow_integral}
\end{equation}
in practice with a discrete solver over a finite step schedule.

To reason about \emph{what the action does} to this flow, we decompose the conditioned field into the zero-command base flow $v_0(x_t,t,c) := v(x_t,t,c,0)$ and an action-driven residual redirection:
\begin{equation}
    d x_t
    =
    \left[
        v_0(x_t,t,c)
        +
        r_\phi(x_t,t,c,a)
    \right]dt.
    \label{eq:residual_action_flow_differential}
\end{equation}
The action $a$ is held fixed during integration, so the ODE evolves only the latent state $x_t$. The effect of changing the action is instead described by the action-space differential of the residual field:
\begin{equation}
    d_a r_\phi(x,t,c,a)
    =
    B_\phi(x,t,c,a)\,da,
    \label{eq:action_differential_residual}
\end{equation}
where $B_\phi$ maps infinitesimal changes in the action coordinates to infinitesimal changes in the velocity field. For a locally linear and additive action space, we approximate this by
\begin{equation}
    r_\phi(x,t,c,a)
    \approx
    B_\phi(x,t,c)a.
    \label{eq:linear_action_redirection}
\end{equation}
In the two-dimensional rover setting, this reads
\begin{equation}
    r_\phi(x,t,c,a)
    =
    a_{\mathrm{fwd}}\, b^{\mathrm{fwd}}_\phi(x,t,c)
    +
    a_{\mathrm{yaw}}\, b^{\mathrm{yaw}}_\phi(x,t,c),
    \label{eq:linear_action_residual}
\end{equation}
where $b^{\mathrm{fwd}}_\phi$ and $b^{\mathrm{yaw}}_\phi$ are scene-conditioned basis vector fields: the first captures how forward motion redirects the generative trajectory, the second how yaw does. Under this model, arbitrary actions are additive combinations of two reusable motion fields rather than separately learned flows.

\paragraph{Realisation.} We emphasise that neither the split into $v_0$ and $r_\phi$ nor the linearity of Eq.~\eqref{eq:linear_action_residual} is enforced architecturally. A single conditioned velocity field $v_\theta(x,t,c,a)$ is realised by adapting the pretrained DiT with low-rank adaptation and injecting $a$ through AdaLN-Zero modulation and per-chunk action tokens. Because the modulation is zero-initialised, the injection is exactly the identity at the start of training---the residual of Eq.~\eqref{eq:residual_action_flow_differential} is exactly zero at initialisation and grows only as the data demands. Thereafter the residual is implicit,
\begin{equation}
    r_\theta(x,t,c,a) \;:=\; v_\theta(x,t,c,a) - v_\theta(x,t,c,0),
    \label{eq:implicit_residual}
\end{equation}
and whether it behaves like the linear model of Eq.~\eqref{eq:linear_action_residual} is an empirical question, which we return to below.

We stress that $r$ is not a module of our system: no separate residual network exists anywhere in the architecture or the losses. It is the \emph{idealised} object---the decomposition an ideal action-conditioned flow would admit---and Eq.~\eqref{eq:implicit_residual} merely gives it an operational definition as a difference of the single learned field. Accordingly, our empirical program is not to inspect a network but to measure a perturbation: the action changes the flow only slightly relative to the flow itself (context and denoising dominate the latent trajectory), yet it changes it \emph{as a whole}---coherently across scenes, noise draws, and denoising steps. It is precisely this small-but-global character that the residual model predicts and that our latent-space analyses test.

Given a training tuple $(c,a,x_1)$ with
\begin{equation}
    x_1 \sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_1 \mid c,a),
\end{equation}
we sample $x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and use the rectified-flow interpolation
\begin{equation}
    x_t=(1-t)x_0+t x_1.
\end{equation}
The action-conditioned training objective is the standard conditional flow-matching loss on the single field,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{flow}}
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{t,x_0,x_1,c,a}
    \left[
        \left\|
        v_\theta(x_t,t,c,a)
        -
        (x_1-x_0)
        \right\|_2^2
    \right],
    \label{eq:action_conditioned_loss}
\end{equation}
optionally augmented with the latent egomotion critic of \S\ref{sec:critic}. At inference time, the action is held fixed and the sample is generated by integrating
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d x_t}{dt}
    =
    v_\theta(x_t,t,c,a),
    \qquad
    x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Predictions of the residual model.} The residual-field model makes three testable predictions. First, \emph{scene-independence}: the same action should redirect the latent trajectory along a consistent direction across different scenes, acting as a relative displacement operator rather than pulling different scenes toward a common destination. Second, \emph{antisymmetry}: opposing commands should produce opposing redirections. Third, \emph{composability}: for short prediction intervals consecutive controls approximately satisfy
\begin{equation}
    a_{1 \rightarrow 3}
    \approx
    a_{1 \rightarrow 2}
    +
    a_{2 \rightarrow 3},
    \label{eq:additive_action_composition}
\end{equation}
and under Eq.~\eqref{eq:linear_action_residual} the residual inherits this additivity,
\begin{equation}
    r_\theta(x,t,c,a+a')
    \approx
    r_\theta(x,t,c,a)
    +
    r_\theta(x,t,c,a'),
    \label{eq:additive_residual}
\end{equation}
so that, e.g., a diagonal command should redirect the flow like the sum of its cardinal components.

\paragraph{Empirical support.} Although nothing enforces the decomposition, the trained model realises most of it. Rolling out held-out contexts under matched initial noise and varying only the command (8 commands $\times$ 32 scenes), the latent displacement induced by a given action correlates positively across scenes (mean same-action displacement cosine $+0.12$ against a cross-action baseline of $-0.02$), opposing commands displace oppositely, and rollout endpoints remain grouped by scene---the action behaves as a \emph{relative} operator on the latent state while the context determines where in latent space the trajectory lives. The operator is strong enough to be read back out: a 5-NN classifier over held-out scenes recovers the commanded direction from the final-chunk latent residual at $71\%$ accuracy (chance $12.5\%$); removing the AdaLN injection collapses this to $31\%$. Composability (Eq.~\eqref{eq:additive_residual}) holds at the level of \emph{realised motion} but not as raw-latent vector addition: diagonal commands realise both cardinal motion components with the total realised magnitude conserved at the commanded magnitude (diagonal-to-cardinal motion-norm ratio $1.08$, against $\sqrt{2}\!\approx\!1.41$ if the axes responded independently), yet the diagonal's latent displacement is not parallel to the sum of the two cardinal displacements (cosine $\approx 0$). The residual composes in the scene's local coordinates, not in a global latent frame---consistent with the scene-relative character of the operator above.

\subsection{Data Derived Action Space}
\label{sec:pca}

Reiterating from the previous section, we require an action space that is grounded (zero motion in the video maps to the zero action, i.e.\ commanded stationarity), smooth, approximately invertible, and linearly composable. In order to get fundamental movement signals, we use a pixel tracking model \cite{cotracker} on a fixed $10\times10$ grid of starting pixels. For every $0.5$s chunk, composed of 3 latent frames which represent 12 pixel frames, we track
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G}=\{p_j\}_{j=1}^{100},
    \qquad
    p_j\in\mathbb{R}^2,
\end{equation}
and compute the displacement of each point:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_j = q_j(T)-q_j(0)\in\mathbb{R}^2.
\end{equation}
We concatenate these into a single movement vector
\begin{equation}
    m =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \delta_1 & \delta_2 & \cdots & \delta_{100}
    \end{bmatrix}^{\top}
    \in \mathbb{R}^{200}.
    \label{eq:movement_vector}
\end{equation}
Some minimal level of time needs to be present in order to capture the action occurring in the frame, but a small enough unit of time such that the actions can be modular and composable in sequences. We thus choose $0.5$ seconds.


When taken over a single chunk, $m$ literally describes how 100 pixels spread across the video move. Semantically, however, these pixels belong to a set of underlying objects, surfaces, or scene regions. Let
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{O}=\{O_\ell\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
\end{equation}
denote the latent set of objects or regions in the frame, and let
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_\ell \subset \{1,\ldots,100\}
\end{equation}
be the set of tracked grid points lying on object $O_\ell$. The average observed motion of object $O_\ell$ over the chunk is then
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\delta}_\ell
    =
    \frac{1}{|\Omega_\ell|}
    \sum_{j\in\Omega_\ell}
    \delta_j.
    \label{eq:object_average_motion}
\end{equation}
Thus the grid motion descriptor can be viewed as a sparse measurement of how a set of scene objects move. In this case, the observed motion of each tracked object contains both egomotion induced motion and scene and object specific motion. We write
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\delta}_{s,n,\ell}
    =
    E_\ell(c_{s,n})\, a_{s,n}
    +
    \rho_{s,n,\ell}
    +
    \varepsilon_{s,n,\ell},
    \label{eq:object_motion_decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $s$ indexes the scene, $n$ indexes the chunk, $a_{s,n}$ is the underlying rover egomotion, $E_\ell(c_{s,n})$ maps this egomotion into the image-plane motion of object $O_\ell$, $\rho_{s,n,\ell}$ is the independent motion of that object, and $\varepsilon_{s,n,\ell}$ is tracking noise.

For a single object, egomotion is ambiguous because $\bar{\delta}_{s,n,\ell}$ depends strongly on depth, object identity, and local motion. However, across many objects, chunks, and scenes, the object-specific terms are not coherent:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}_{s,n,\ell}[\rho_{s,n,\ell}]
    \approx 0,
    \qquad
    \mathbb{E}_{s,n,\ell}[\varepsilon_{s,n,\ell}]
    \approx 0.
\end{equation}
The egomotion term, in contrast, induces a shared correlated pattern across the tracked points. Stacking all object or grid-point motions gives
\begin{equation}
    m_{s,n}
    =
    E(c_{s,n})a_{s,n}
    +
    \rho_{s,n}
    +
    \varepsilon_{s,n},
    \label{eq:stacked_motion_decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $E(c_{s,n})$ is the scene-dependent egomotion-to-image-motion operator. After centering,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde m_{s,n}=m_{s,n}-m_0,
\end{equation}
the covariance of the movement vectors is
\begin{equation}
    C
    =
    \mathbb{E}\left[\tilde m_{s,n}\tilde m_{s,n}^{\top}\right].
\end{equation}
Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:stacked_motion_decomposition}, and assuming the object-specific residuals are weakly correlated with egomotion, gives
\begin{equation}
    C
    \approx
    \mathbb{E}\left[
        E(c)a a^\top E(c)^\top
    \right]
    +
    \Sigma_{\rho}
    +
    \Sigma_{\varepsilon}.
    \label{eq:motion_covariance_decomposition}
\end{equation}
The first term is the shared egomotion-induced covariance, while $\Sigma_{\rho}$ and $\Sigma_{\varepsilon}$ correspond to object-specific motion and tracking noise. When motion is aggregated over many chunks and scenes, the incoherent residual terms are spread across many directions, while egomotion contributes coherent high-variance directions. PCA therefore recovers the dominant shared motion components:
\begin{equation}
    C u_k = \lambda_k u_k.
\end{equation}

This is the sense in which individual diversions disappear and egomotion emerges. A single tracked point or object is scene dependent, but the common reflex motion across many objects and scenes produces a stable low-dimensional subspace. We use the top two PCA directions as the action input,
\begin{equation}
    a_i = U_2^\top \tilde m_i,
    \qquad
    U_2=[u_1,u_2],
\end{equation}
while retaining a larger PCA subspace for supervision:
\begin{equation}
    z_i = U_r^\top \tilde m_i,
    \qquad
    \hat m_i = m_0 + U_r z_i.
\end{equation}
Thus, the two-dimensional action captures the dominant shared egomotion, while the retained PCA code $z$ (with $U_r=[u_1,\ldots,u_r]$ chosen to explain approximately $90\%$ of the motion variance) preserves a cleaner and approximately invertible representation of the full tracked motion, used for supervision in \S\ref{sec:critic}.

Using PCA also ensures that the components are additive and composable. For two short chunks,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde m_{1:2}\approx \tilde m_1+\tilde m_2,
\end{equation}
and by linearity,
\begin{equation}
    a_{1:2}=U_2^\top \tilde m_{1:2}
    \approx a_1+a_2.
\end{equation}
Alternatively, when there is no overall motion, $\tilde m_i\approx0$, so the PCA components also correspond to near $0$. This also means that action dimensions that are \emph{not} present in the video \emph{cannot} be recovered by this method as it is entirely data driven. Conversely, actions which are present but imbalanced can still be recovered if they form a coherent direction of variance: the principal component corresponding to yawing left will be the inverted component of yawing right, for example.

In Frodobots data, the leading 2 components emerge as the forward/backward dimension (holds \~60\% of the variance) and the yaw dimension (holds \~20\% of the variance).

\subsection{Latent Egomotion Critic without Decoder Gradients}
\label{sec:critic}

Action conditioning alone does not guarantee that the model uses the action. A rectified-flow objective can be minimized by predicting a likely continuation while partially ignoring the command. We therefore add an egomotion grounding loss which evaluates whether the generated latent video actually renders the commanded motion.

A naive solution would be to decode generated latents to pixels, run the same pixel-tracking pipeline used to define the PCA action space, compare the measured motion to the command, and backpropagate through both the decoder and the tracker. This is undesirable both computationally and as an optimization target. Computationally, in our setting, differentiating through decoded pixels expands the loss interface from \(21\times16\times60\times104\) latent values to \(81\times3\times480\times832\) RGB values, a \(46\times\) increase before accounting for the additional VAE decoder and tracker activations. Optimization-wise, gradients through pixel tracking can encourage shortcut pixel-moving solutions rather than coherent holistic egomotion. We therefore do not pass generator gradients through the decoder or tracking pipeline. Instead, we use this visual pipeline only as a frozen no-gradient teacher, and distil it online into a lightweight latent-space critic.

Let \(c\) denote the clean latent context, \(a\in\mathbb{R}^2\) the two-dimensional PCA action used for conditioning, and \(z\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\) the retained PCA motion code used for supervision. The first two dimensions of \(z\) correspond to the action input,
\begin{equation}
    a = P_2 z,
\end{equation}
where \(P_2:\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\) selects the top two PCA dimensions. With rectified-flow interpolation
\begin{equation}
    x_\tau=(1-\tau)x_0+\tau x_1,
    \qquad
    x_0\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),
\end{equation}
the generator is trained with the standard action-conditioned flow objective $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{flow}}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:action_conditioned_loss}. During training, the model also produces a clean latent prediction \(\hat{x}_0\). For a rectified-flow model this can be estimated from the current noisy latent and predicted velocity as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{x}_0
    =
    x_\tau + (1-\tau)v_\theta(x_\tau,\tau,c,a),
    \label{eq:predicted_clean_latent}
\end{equation}
or obtained from a short sampled rollout.

We define a frozen visual egomotion teacher using the same pipeline as in the previous section. Given a generated latent video \(\hat{x}_0\), the teacher decodes it, runs the pixel tracker, constructs the movement vector, and projects it into the retained PCA subspace:
\begin{equation}
    T(\hat{x}_0)
    =
    U_r^\top
    \left(
        M\left(
            \mathrm{Track}
            \left(
                \mathrm{Dec}(\hat{x}_0)
            \right)
        \right)
        -
        m_0
    \right)
    \in\mathbb{R}^{r},
    \label{eq:visual_teacher}
\end{equation}
where \(M(\cdot)\) denotes the construction of the \(200\)-dimensional movement vector from the tracked grid. This gives the PCA motion code rendered by the current generated sample. Crucially, this teacher is evaluated under stop-gradient:
\begin{equation}
    z^{\mathrm{teach}} = T(\mathrm{sg}(\hat{x}_0)).
    \label{eq:teacher_readout}
\end{equation}
Thus, the decoder, tracker, and PCA projection are forward-only label machinery.

We train a lightweight latent critic \(C_\phi\) to imitate this teacher directly in latent space. The critic takes the detached generated clean latents, the current flow timestep, and the commanded action, and predicts the retained PCA motion code:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{z}_{\phi}
    =
    C_\phi(\mathrm{sg}(\hat{x}_0),\tau,a).
\end{equation}
It is optimized with a weighted regression loss
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{crit}}
    =
    \left\|
        W_c
        \left(
            C_\phi(\mathrm{sg}(\hat{x}_0),\tau,a)
            -
            \mathrm{sg}(z^{\mathrm{teach}})
        \right)
    \right\|_2^2 .
    \label{eq:critic_loss}
\end{equation}
The critic is trained online on the current generator distribution: it is not trained in a separate pass over real videos. Real data enters through the PCA basis fitted on real trajectories and through the pretrained tracker. The timestep input is included because \(\hat{x}_0\) varies in quality across the denoising trajectory; early predictions can be blurrier or less stable than late predictions.

Generator guidance then freezes the critic, or uses an exponential-moving-average copy \(C_{\bar{\phi}}\), and applies it to the non-detached generated latents:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{z}_{\mathrm{gen}}
    =
    C_{\bar{\phi}}(\hat{x}_0,\tau,a).
\end{equation}
The generator is encouraged to produce latents whose critic-predicted motion matches the commanded PCA code:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}}
    =
    \left\|
        W_g
        \left(
            C_{\bar{\phi}}(\hat{x}_0,\tau,a)
            -
            z
        \right)
    \right\|_2^2 .
    \label{eq:latent_action_loss}
\end{equation}
The weighting \(W_g\) places full weight on the two commanded action dimensions and lower weight on the remaining retained PCA dimensions, which regularize the generated motion without overwhelming the primary control objective.

The generator gradient therefore follows only the latent critic path
\begin{equation}
    \theta
    \rightarrow
    \hat{x}_0
    \rightarrow
    C_{\bar{\phi}}(\hat{x}_0,\tau,a)
    \rightarrow
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}},
\end{equation}
and never follows the visual teacher path
\begin{equation}
    \theta
    \rightarrow
    \hat{x}_0
    \rightarrow
    \mathrm{Dec}(\hat{x}_0)
    \rightarrow
    \mathrm{Track}
    \rightarrow
    \mathrm{PCA}.
\end{equation}
The critic therefore supervises the generated latents directly to be read as the commanded motion; it does not supervise a difference between generated and real latents.

The full generator objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gen}}
    =
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{flow}}
    +
    \lambda_{\mathrm{act}}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}}.
    \label{eq:generator_objective}
\end{equation}
The critic objective \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{crit}}\) updates only \(\phi\). Thus, the decoder and tracker provide an online visual teacher of rendered egomotion, but they never receive or transmit generator gradients. The critic distils this teacher into latent space, giving the generator a cheap differentiable action loss while preserving the stability and efficiency of the pretrained flow model.

We borrow this trick from GAN literature which trains the discriminator on local gradients to aid in stability while providing an online discriminative signal. We find that this approach works to add dense supervision signal that is constantly shifting, leading to new gradients on old data, refreshing the limited amount (~6\%) of backward data that we have with new movement signal.

\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\paragraph{Data.} We train on the FrodoBots dataset as previously mentioned. We subselect in this dataset using a three-city subset (Madrid, Rome, Stockholm): $574$ rides, $81.5$ hours, $\sim\!70$K streaming windows. Four held-out rides (one per city plus an extra) are used for evaluation. All models are trained from the frozen base Wan weights; ablations are run for $4{,}000$ steps each (controllability saturates well before this).

\paragraph{An independent controllability metric.} No standard action-following metric exists for this setting, and the natural candidates---our critic and probe---are trained \emph{inside} the model, so any score routed through them is circular. We therefore define a \emph{counterfactual swap} protocol scored only by frozen, externally pretrained modules. For each held-out clip we fix the clean context and the initial sampling noise and regenerate the continuation under four commands: \textsc{true} (the real egomotion), \textsc{zero} (no motion), \textsc{flip} ($-a$), and \textsc{shuffle} (time-permuted). We then read the \emph{rendered} egomotion of each generation with the frozen CoTracker$\rightarrow$ss-VAE pipeline (untrained by the run) and compare it to the command. Reusing the initial noise across conditions isolates the causal effect of the command. We report Pearson correlation between rendered and commanded egomotion, per control axis, averaged over rides and seeds.

\subsection{The Model Is Controllable (Independently Verified)}
Table~\ref{tab:cond} reports correlation between the rendered egomotion and the \emph{true} command across conditions, under the frozen judge. For the full model the rendered motion tracks the true command ($0.74$ pooled across axes) and \emph{deviates} when the command is corrupted---the counterfactual signature of genuine control: \textsc{flip} drops the correlation to $0.14$ and \textsc{zero} to $0.67$. Broken out by axis (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), the full model follows steering at $r{=}0.945$ and throttle at $r{=}0.751$. These numbers use no trained component in the judge.

\subsection{What Makes It Controllable: Ablations}
We perform leave-one-out ablations, removing one mechanism at a time and scoring each with the same frozen judge (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\paragraph{Two of the four mechanisms carry control; the other two are redundant.} Removing AdaLN (action tokens only) collapses steering control from $0.945$ to $0.578$, and the model stops reacting to commands: its correlation with the true command is \emph{flat} across \textsc{true}/\textsc{zero}/\textsc{flip} (Table~\ref{tab:cond}, all $\approx0.73$), i.e.\ the output is invariant to the command. Removing the output-level critic guidance is similarly damaging (steering $0.433$, flat counterfactual row). In contrast, removing either the per-frame action tokens ($0.894$ steering, command still causal) or the representation-level state probe ($0.910$ steering, \textsc{flip} still drops the correlation to $0.20$) leaves a model close to the full system. Thus controllability is carried by \emph{AdaLN modulation and output-level critic guidance}, while action tokens and the state-probe grounding are largely redundant---a result that simplifies the method. Notably, a circular metric computed from the model's own trained probe \emph{early in training} suggested the opposite ranking among the injection channels; the frozen-judge protocol reverses it, underscoring why an independent metric is necessary.

\subsection{Dimensionality of the Action Representation}
Both grounding heads are trained against the full $8$-D realized-motion latent, with the two commanded axes (steering $z_2$, throttle $z_7$) up-weighted and the remaining six dimensions encoding scene motion that is not directly commanded. We test whether those six dimensions contribute to control. \emph{Probe-2D} reduces the state probe's target to $z_2/z_7$ only (compare to the full model); \emph{Critic-8D} instead \emph{adds} the six dimensions to the action critic---which otherwise grounds only $z_2/z_7$---at half the guidance weight of the commanded axes (compare to ``$-$ State probe'', whose critic already uses $z_2/z_7$ only). These two runs were stopped once controllability had saturated and are therefore trained for fewer steps than the leave-one-out models (Table~\ref{tab:dims}, ``Steps''); the comparison is indicative rather than budget-matched.

\subsection{Limitation: Reverse Egomotion}
Stratifying the throttle axis by command direction reveals a systematic failure. All models render \emph{forward} motion with the correct sign on $100\%$ of forward chunks, but \emph{reverse} commands are rendered poorly: the full model produces the correct (backward) sign on only $46.7\%$ of reverse chunks and strongly under-shoots the commanded magnitude. The model has a forward-motion prior it does not readily override. This also explains an apparent per-scene anomaly: the held-out Madrid ride is reverse-dominated, and its low controllability is the reverse-rendering failure rather than a scene- or alignment-specific artifact.

\subsection{Visual Quality and Drift}
We complement the controllability analysis with a visual-quality testbench, aligned with the metrics common to the autoregressive-video lineage. For each model we sample $48$ windowed clips across the held-out rides, generate the continuation under the true command, and report: \emph{prediction fidelity} against the ground-truth video (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS); \emph{intrinsic quality} on the generated clip (MUSIQ for imaging quality, NIMA for aesthetics, CLIP feature consistency across frames, and temporal flicker---the same dimensions VBench reports); and a distributional FVD. As VBench and an I3D model are not used here, FVD is computed from r3d\_18 (Kinetics) features and is \emph{indicative} (relative ordering only). We also report a \emph{short-rollout drift} arm: the per-frame slope of fidelity (PSNR), perceptual error (LPIPS) and intrinsic quality (MUSIQ) across the rollout, plus statistical brightness/contrast drift---the error-accumulation and over-saturation that this lineage flags. (Long-horizon drift is out of scope.)

Two observations follow. First, raw visual quality is nearly uniform across all variants, and where it differs it moves \emph{opposite} to controllability: the fully grounded model (ours) has the lowest fidelity and the lowest indicative FVD-rank, while removing the critic guidance yields the best quality (PSNR $19.98$, LPIPS $0.210$, FVD $21.8$) precisely because it is freed from the command-following constraint that, as shown above, it needs for control. \section{Conclusion}
\emph{[Draft stub --- to be completed.]} We presented an action-grounding objective that makes a pretrained autoregressive video diffusion model obey egomotion commands, and a frozen-judge protocol that measures controllability without circularity. Ablations attribute control to AdaLN modulation and output-level critic guidance---while per-frame action tokens and the representation-level state probe are largely redundant \emph{for control}, though the action tokens separately serve as a spatiotemporal memory that preserves scene content under camera motion---and show that this control is bought at a small, measurable cost in visual fidelity, with negligible short-rollout drift. The principal open problem is the systematic failure to render reverse egomotion.
